Jamie Edwards is an accomplished executive who brings more than two decades of global infrastructure investment and development expertise to his role as Managing Director Resources for Origis Energy. He is responsible for the asset management of Origis owned solar and storage projects.

Mr. Edwards’ career spans a wide range of traditional and renewable energy asset types including solar, wind, hydro, and thermal generation as well as transmission and distribution, telecommunications, transportation, and water projects and investments for C&I clients, investor-owned, municipal, and state-owned utilities.

Investment supports 413 MWdc of new solar capacity in Texas, advancing grid resilience, local tax revenue, and long-term energy affordability.
New York, NY (February 5, 2026) — (EIN Presswire) — Today Crux, the capital platform for the clean economy, and Origis Energy, one of America’s leading renewable energy and decarbonization platforms, announced an investment of $340 million in tax equity capital to support a 413 MWdc utility-scale solar project in Ector County, Texas.
The project is expected to reach commercial operations in the second half of 2026. It will advance local energy affordability and resilience in West Texas — generating over 900GWh of solar energy annually for the local grid, which equates to powering 85,000 homes or a city of 250,000 people — and is projected to drive $68M of property tax revenue for Ector County.
This tax equity investment, backed by investment grade-rated capital, was underwritten and structured by Crux Capital Securities, Crux’s registered broker-dealer. The investment is made through a Crux-managed vehicle with Origis Energy as a hybrid partnership flip designed to optimize monetization of its federal investment tax credits (ITC) through the use of transferability.
Tax equity is a critical financing tool in the US renewable energy market, enabling developers to efficiently deploy capital and advance resilient energy infrastructure. Hybrid tax equity strategies make up the majority of tax equity investments, and transfers out of tax equity are the fastest-growing part of the market. Crux’s market data estimates that hybrid tax equity structures, which are structured to transfer a portion of the tax credits, made up more than 75% of all tax equity investments in 2025.
“This investment reflects how the tax equity market is evolving toward more integrated, flexible structures that unlock capital efficiently and at scale,” said Yonette Chung McLean, Head of Investments and Crux Capital Securities. “Origis Energy is a best-in-class developer building high-quality projects. By bringing together tax equity, transferability, and investment-grade capital within a single structure, Crux was proud to support Origis in advancing a project that will deliver long-term value for Texas communities and the broader grid.”
“This investment enables Origis to deliver a large-scale solar project that strengthens grid resilience, supports long-term energy affordability, and generates substantial local tax revenue,” said Alice Heathcote, Chief Financial Officer of Origis Energy. “Thank you to Crux for their sustainable tax equity solutions. We value the strong partnership as we continue to invest in high-impact renewable infrastructure across Texas and the United States.”
Crux combines authoritative market intelligence, a broad range of capital products, and the leading team in the market, to accelerate capital deployment into clean energy. Crux has assembled a highly experienced team of energy finance professionals across debt, tax credits and tax equity, providing streamlined execution, underwriting, tax credit transfers, and fund-level management, creating efficiency versus one-off transactions. This investment establishes a repeatable framework for future investments as Origis Energy continues to expand its renewable energy portfolio.
For more information on Crux, please visit https://cruxclimate.com/. For more information on Origis Energy, please visit https://origisenergy.com/.
Crux is the capital platform for the clean economy. Crux’s expert team, authoritative market intelligence, and AI-powered platform are changing the way energy developers, manufacturers, and investors raise and deploy the capital needed to power a more prosperous, clean, and secure energy future. Crux facilitates transactions across the full capital stack, including transferable tax credits, debt capital, and tax and preferred equity, and has supported the raising and deployment of billions of dollars in clean energy capital for its clients. Since its launch in 2023, Crux has raised more than $77 million in funding from venture capital and strategic investors.

February 25, 2025 | Josh Corbitt, Vice President of Business Development at Origis Energy Services, will join a panel on Extreme Weather Resiliency, focusing on optimizing system hardening investments to manage risk in the solar industry.
Origis Energy Services is proud to sponsor Infocast’s ERCOT Market Summit 2025, scheduled for February 25-27 in Austin, TX. Josh Corbitt, Vice President of Business Development, will participate in the panel discussion on “Extreme Weather Resiliency—Optimizing System Hardening Investments to Manage Risk.” The summit will cover key topics such as resource adequacy, grid upgrades, power prices, project siting, interconnection, and bankability, focusing on the evolving needs of commercial, industrial, and retail customers.
Even before Beryl touched down, Texas utilities were developing approaches to hardening the grid against the risks of wildfires, flooding, hurricane force winds, and other extreme weather events. The costs of system hardening can be extremely high and must be carefully planned. This panel will explore the options utilities are choosing to harden their systems to increase reliability and resiliency and the consequences of that hardening on the rest of power system.

Josh Corbitt of Origis Energy Services will be available to discuss the insightful poster presentation titled “Getting Real About the Cost of Vegetation Management.” Don’t miss this opportunity to explore the real-world challenges and solutions in vegetation management for solar operations, and gain valuable perspectives from an industry expert at RE+ 25 in Las Vegas, NV.
Location:
V3275, Level 1, Venetian Expo Hall
Poster Room Appearance:
Tuesday, September 9, 2025 at 4:00 pm PT
Poster Synopsis: Vegetation management can be a large and variable cost, impacting the ROI of a project. Unpredictable costs can occur particularly in the transfer of a project from the construction EPC crew to the long-term O&M operator. This poster outlines the areas in which vegetation management costs can add up and planning solutions to keep costs in line. It is relevant for asset owners, managers, EPC and developers. Elements that impact the budget for vegetation management include site design and engineering, site preparation, seeding including seed mix and appropriateness for local biodiversity, water management, and long-term management, labor cost and availability, equipment selection for vegetation management; including consideration of natural trimmers like sheep and the match of mowing equipment to the types of vegetation, the condition of site upon transfer to O&M and view shed and impacts on local communities and biodiversity.

Episode #23 of Power Players by Origis® features Joe Rand, Energy Policy Researcher in the Energy Markets and Policy Department at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and Glenna Wiseman, Head of Marketing and Communications for Origis Energy
In Episode 23 of Power Players by Origis®, host Glenna Wiseman, Head of Marketing and Communications for Origis Energy and Joe Rand, Energy Policy Researcher in the Energy Markets and Policy Department at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, discuss community engagement strategies for developing renewable energy projects.
Joe Rand is an Energy Policy Researcher in the Energy Markets and Policy Department at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Joe conducts research and analysis on renewable energy, including renewable energy policy, cost, and market analysis; spatial data analysis; and research related to social impacts, interconnection, and deployment barriers of renewable energy.
Glenna Wiseman has nearly two decades of renewable energy experience as Head of Marketing & Communications for Origis Energy. A discipline for inclusive stakeholder engagement drives her long-standing determination to amplify customer stories and build a strong company brand.
Wiseman and Rand began the conversation discussing Rand’s research. The groundbreaking Lawrence Berkeley lab report, called “Halfway Up the Ladder,” documents responses from 62 companies. The report outlines developer concerns about opposition, typical strategy pivot points made in response to opposition, and potential paths forward to greater community collaboration.
Rand and his colleagues have been studying the impact of renewable energy projects on the communities that host them for years. And while there are quite a few benefits from these projects, there are also some perceived negative implications as well. While there is considerable data from various stakeholder groups from neighbors to public officials on the benefits or drawbacks of projects, there has not been data from the point of view of the developer. The team felt that their point of view was necessary to fully understand the barriers to clean energy development and deployment.
“We began to understand, we [as an industry] could build projects at lower cost and potentially more quickly if we more proactively address the concerns and met the needs of host communities,” said Rand. “We felt it was critical to analyze this question from the developer’s point of view, not just from community members, and to really try to untangle some of those complexities.”
The reference to “halfway up the ladder” comes from research done in the 60s known as “Arnstein’s Ladder.” It’s one of the most established and widely used frameworks for assessing and understanding community engagement in the planning and community development literature and research space. Most of the developers surveyed in the research Rand conducted say halfway up the ladder is the appropriate way to engage the public in planning.
Rand made clear this is not a judgement of where any developer should be on the ladder, but a statement of where many developers currently are.
Rand mentioned permit reform is going on across jurisdictions around the country. Many in the renewable energy sector would like to streamline siting and permitting processes.
From the research, developers liked state-level permitting because it was more predictable and gave more certainty to a project. However, those same developers believed communities receive more benefits from local-based permitting.
“This is one of those areas where it’s important to draw the data out and think about the implications of how we think about permitting reform and streamlining and benefits to the community,” Rand said. “If we really want to move to state-based siting and permitting, let’s make sure we’re not doing it at the expense of net benefits to hosting communities. So, how do we get the best of both worlds?”
Wiseman agreed good developers are looking to find a way to give certainty to investors in projects while at the same time bringing benefits to communities and finding the best ways to communicate with those communities at the appropriate times.
“I think there are a lot of developers who take [local] concerns into consideration, create the pivots you outline in the study to modify projects as needed and are able to move forward with something that is more collaborative,” said Wiseman. “I also think that there’s a growing awareness in the industry around the types of opposition, true community opposition versus AstroTurf opposition.”
Rand acknowledged while there are known sources of misinformation and disinformation, it can be very difficult to sort out what are genuine local, grassroots concerns and what’s been blurred by outside interests.
They had done previous research that indicated it was more important to have a true representative stakeholder engagement process rather than just a typical town hall type meeting. He suggested formats to bring more stakeholders to the table rather than just those who were very supportive or strongly opposed.
Rand referenced a study of residents who lived near utility-scale wind energy projects as an example. Overall, attitudes were largely positive or indifferent with a very small minority who held a negative attitude about the projects. But when it came to who showed up to town hall meetings, the speakers tended to almost always be 50/50 split on positive and negative sentiment, which is not an accurate depiction of the community’s overall feelings about the project.
Rand reiterated it’s very important to address local concerns and adjust projects as needed. However, it’s also important for policy makers and local leaders to have an accurate picture of the true sentiment in their communities.
“One of the things we’re trying to do through this research and other projects we have going on at Berkeley Lab is support a more representative and democratic process and better information sharing so it’s not just this minority voice that can dominate the conversation,” said Rand.
Wiseman and Rand concluded positive, respectful community engagement by developers is the best path forward.

A groundswell of local opposition to permitting renewable energy continues to plague the development of solar and other renewables, making it increasingly likely that projects will encounter difficulties siting in otherwise suitable areas. Many groups and constituencies have expressed a range of concerns with solar, wind, and storage projects. In response many national organizations and private companies are developing methods to court vital constituencies, combat disinformation campaigns, and educate local government officials.
This panel will examine the sources of local opposition, and progress to date on developing effective methods to overcome these hurdles.

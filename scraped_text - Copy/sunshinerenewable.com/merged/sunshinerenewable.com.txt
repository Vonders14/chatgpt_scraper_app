Texas solar is not winding down. It is settling into its second phase, the part where things get efficient and financially straightforward. The early days of solar in Texas were about proof of concept. Projects built 20 or 30 years ago faced higher equipment costs/permitting constraints, and far more skeptical lenders. Developers had to explain solar to uninformed crowds with slide decks and patience.
Today, the conversation has changed. Equipment costs have dropped sharply, and interconnection processes are better understood. Approvals move faster because utilities/regulators and financiers have seen these projects perform.
One of the most persistent misconceptions we hear is that the best days of Texas solar have sailed away. In reality, barriers to entry are lower than ever. Modern modules produce more power per acre and construction timelines are shorter because supply chains/contractors are experienced.
For commercial solar and C&I solar projects, this means fewer surprises between design and commissioning. Financing is more flexible, with structures that align well with different balance sheets and risk profiles; this is why many businesses are viewing solar not as a one-time sustainability statement but an investment opportunity. It is less about rushing to beat a clock and more about making a rational allocation decision.
Another concern is that incentives are disappearing – making solar less attractive. Incentives do change, but they rarely vanish overnight, and the economics of solar no longer depend on a single policy lever. Even as programs adjust, solar remains a hedge against rising, volatile energy costs.
Locking in a portion of long-term power supply through on-site generation or contracted projects provides cost certainty that is difficult to replicate. For large-scale solar customers, this often translates into reduced exposure to peak pricing and more predictable operating expenses. Renewable energy investments work best when they are evaluated over their full lifecycle, not just through the lens of the current incentive landscape.
The second phase of Texas solar is less about proving viability and more about optimizing outcomes. That is where experienced guidance matters.

Businesses that install solar can still qualify for a federal tax credit covering around 30% of total project costs. There is potential for even greater savings depending on project location and energy community status.
To claim the full 30% credit, projects placed in service after 2025 must include at least 40% U.S.-manufactured components. Equipment from China, Russia, Iran, or North Korea is no longer eligible. Non-compliant projects may lose part or all of their credit eligibility.
Businesses can typically recover a large portion of their solar investment through accelerated depreciation, allowing them to deduct much of the system’s value over the first several years; this tax advantage reduces upfront payback time and strengthens long-term project ROI.
Energy-storage systems such as batteries will remain eligible for the 30% tax credit with no concrete cutoff date currently planned.
Solar installations built in designated “energy communities” areas transitioning from coal, oil, or natural gas production may qualify for additional federal savings of up to 10%.
Through the Rural Energy for America Program (REAP), agricultural producers and rural small businesses can apply for grants covering up to 50% of their solar project costs—dramatically reducing out-of-pocket expenses.

Most solar energy systems installed today are standard, grid-tied systems. ‘Grid-tied’ means that the power the system produces bypasses the building it’s installed on, and goes straight into the power grid. Your energy consumption comes from the grid as it always has, and is weighed by your retail energy provider against the amount that your solar system contributes to the grid (see next question for more details). The alternative to a standard, grid-tied system is a hybrid, grid-tied system. The difference is simply some additional hardware, including an automatic transfer switch, a charge controller, and batteries for storage. Whether installed initially or added as a retrofit to an existing standard, grid-tied system, this additional hardware will allow you to draw power from your system in the event of a grid outage. Due to the high cost of existing battery technology, most solar purchasers today decide against hybrid systems—opting instead for standard systems, with the option to add the hybrid hardware at a later date, when battery technologies have improved in efficiency and cost. Sunshine Renewable Solutions offers a wide range of hardware configurations for both standard and hybrid systems.
If you have the right plan, yes! What you’ll want to look for is a 1-to-1 buyback program. With a 1-to-1 buyback plan, your retail energy provider (REP) will remotely monitor both your contribution to the grid (total power generated by your system) and your total consumption from the grid through a process called net metering. The net balance will be purchased at one predetermined price-per-kWh by either you or your REP. For example, let’s say you’re on such a plan at a fixed 12¢/kWh. During a billing cycle in which you consume a total of 1,500 kWh and produce a total of 1,300 kWh, you would pay a bill of $24. If instead, your production exceeded your consumption by the same 200 kWh, your REP would credit your account $24. At the time of this writing, the Houston-area REPs who offer 1-to-1 buyback programs are Reliant Energy and Green Mountain Energy.

The plant is famous for concentrating solar power (CSP) technology; this is accomplished through tracking mirrors known as heliostats that direct solar power onto boilers. The boilers create steam that initiates turbines that produce electricity. Since its inception in 2014, the plant has developed a gross capacity of 377 megawatts (MW).
